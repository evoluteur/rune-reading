Rune Reading

About runes

Runes are the letters of the early Germanic peoples. The word meant “secret” or “whisper”: the letters were used for writing, for carving names and charms on objects, and, in later tradition, for divination.

Carved, not written

Runes were made to be cut into hard surfaces: wood, bone, antler, stone and metal. That is why they are built from straight strokes, mostly vertical staves with short slanting branches, and why they avoid long horizontal lines that would follow the grain of a wooden stick and split it. Most runic writing was on wood, and almost all of that has rotted away, so what survives today is mostly stone, metal and bone.

Most inscriptions are short and practical: the owner of a comb or a spearhead, the maker of a brooch, the name of someone remembered on a memorial stone. Some are charms or curses. Each rune also had a name, a whole word, so a single carved letter could stand for a sound or, for a reader who knew the tradition, for the idea in its name.

The Elder Futhark

The oldest runic alphabet has 24 runes and is named from its first six letters, F, U, Th, A, R and K, just as “alphabet” is named from alpha and beta. It is traditionally split into three groups of eight, the aettir (singular aett, roughly “family”), named for the first rune of each: Freyr’s, Hagal’s and Tyr’s. Every rune stands for a sound and also carries a word that names it:

Fehu
The sound F, and the word for cattle, and so for wealth.
Raidho
The sound R, and the word for riding, and so for a journey.
Othala
The sound O, and the word for inherited land, and so for home and heritage.

Old runes you can still see

The runes in this app are drawn from the Elder Futhark, but real inscriptions from the period are still in museums and fields across Northern Europe. A few well-known ones, with approximate dates:

  • c. 160 CEThe Vimose comb, found in a bog on the Danish island of Funen, carries the word harja and is among the earliest known runic inscriptions.
  • c. 400 CEThe Kylver stone, from Gotland in Sweden, was set in a grave and has the whole futhark cut into it in order. See photos.
  • 6th centuryThe Vadstena bracteate, a thin gold medallion from Sweden, has the futhark stamped around its edge, divided into the three groups of eight. See photos.
  • late 7th centuryThe Björketorp stone, in Blekinge, Sweden, is a tall standing stone whose inscription warns against anyone who would disturb the monument. See photos.
  • c. 800 CEThe Rök runestone, in Sweden, has the longest known runic inscription on a stone, hundreds of characters in the later 16-rune alphabet. See photos.

Beyond the Elder Futhark

Runes changed with the people who used them. Around 800 CE, in Scandinavia, the alphabet shrank to 16 runes, the Younger Futhark, as the spoken language changed, and one rune then had to cover several sounds. In England and Frisia the Anglo-Saxons went the other way and grew the alphabet to more than 30 runes, the futhorc. Rune Reading uses the Elder Futhark, the oldest and most widely used set for modern divination.

A short history

  • c. 98 CETacitus, in the Germania, describes Germanic peoples casting marked twigs to read omens. He does not mention runes.
  • c. 160 CESome of the earliest known runic inscriptions, such as the one on a comb found at Vimose in Denmark, date from about this time.
  • 5th centuryThe Kylver stone from Gotland carries the whole futhark in order.
  • c. 800In Scandinavia the alphabet shrinks to the 16-rune Younger Futhark, while the Anglo-Saxons grow theirs into the futhorc.
  • 13th centuryThe Poetic Edda, as recorded in Iceland, tells in the Hávamál how Odin hung nine nights on the world tree and took up the runes.
  • 1982Ralph Blum’s The Book of Runes popularizes rune casting in English. It adds a blank rune that has no ancient precedent.

What is known and what is revived

No ancient description of how runes were cast for divination survives. Modern rune reading is a revival, built on the rune names, on the medieval Old English, Norwegian and Icelandic rune poems, and on twentieth-century practice. That does not make it less useful as a way to reflect on a question, but it is worth knowing which parts are old.

How this app uses them

Each reading draws runes from the bag of 24 without replacement, so a rune never appears twice. Every rune has its name, its sound (click the speaker icon on a drawn rune to hear its name), its old meaning, and a short upright and reversed reading written for this app. Treat a reading as a mirror for your own judgment rather than a verdict.
